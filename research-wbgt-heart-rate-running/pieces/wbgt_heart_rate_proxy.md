# WBGT as a Proxy for Running Heart Rate

**Takeaway:** WBGT is a heat-stress *index*, not a heart-rate predictor. Rank your candidate run windows by WBGT, pick the lowest, and you get the lowest cardiovascular strain for a given effort. WBGT cannot by itself output a runner's beats per minute.

## 1. Why heat elevates heart rate during running
During exercise the heart must supply **both** working muscle and skin. At maximal effort ~80–85% of cardiac output already goes to muscle [8]. As core temperature rises, the thermoregulatory response drives **cutaneous vasodilation and skin blood flow** to shed heat — "hyperthermia occurs when muscle-generated heat accumulates faster than it dissipates via increased sweating and skin blood flow" [8], a mechanism grounded in Westerholm's athlete work [3]. Skin and muscle compete for the same cardiac output, and dehydration shrinks plasma volume, so the heart raises rate to hold output (CO = SV × HR) [4]. Controlled trials confirm it: at a fixed effort, HR climbs with WBGT (one study found ~5–10 bpm higher at equal WBGT in hot-dry vs. warm-wet air) [11].

## 2. Is there a direct WBGT → HR equation?
No. There is no validated population equation mapping WBGT to a runner's HR at fixed effort. WBGT is an *environmental* composite (outdoor: 0.7·T_wetbulb + 0.2·T_globe + 0.1·T_drybulb). It is deployed as a categorical risk flag, not a point predictor, and the HR-vs-WBGT slope is individual. NIOSH therefore pairs WBGT screening with **physiological monitoring** — heart rate, core temperature, body-water loss — because the index alone cannot predict an individual's strain [4].

## 3. The standard approach: WBGT as a heat-strain proxy
Use WBGT as a proxy for cardiovascular strain. ACSM shows exertional-heat-illness incidence "rises as the WBGT rises" [8]: same distance, same effort, far more collapse at high WBGT. **Action: choose the lowest-WBGT window** — all else equal, HR-for-effort and heat-illness risk are lowest then.

## What WBGT cannot model (needs runner physiology)
Without per-runner data you cannot estimate absolute HR: age (NIOSH: >60 at higher risk), weight/BMI (obesity raises heat-disorder risk), fitness (VO₂max), acclimatization (unacclimatized runners hit higher HR and collapse sooner [4]), hydration, pace/effort, clothing, and medications. WBGT is environment-only — **pair it with a runner's own baseline HR** to make it actionable.
